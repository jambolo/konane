# Cultural guidelines

Kōnane is a Hawaiian game, and the people who open this app are here partly because of that. The brand's job is to present the game accurately and stay out of its way. These rules matter more than any color or typeface in this folder.

## Do

- Describe kōnane as a Hawaiian game in its own right: "Kōnane is a Hawaiian strategy game."
- Spell Hawaiian words correctly, every time. See [voice.md](voice.md#hawaiian-spelling).
- Use the game's own words for its own things, and explain them once.
- Choose colors, images, icons, and typefaces that have a Hawaiian source, and be able to name it. [visual.md](visual.md) gives the source for each.
- Show the real materials: lava stone, coral, basalt.
- Say only what a source supports. The list below is what we can stand behind today.
- Name the source when stating a fact about history or language.
- Write about the game in the present tense. People play kōnane now.

## Don't

- No "Hawaiian checkers", and no saying kōnane resembles checkers or any other game. It does not. Explain it by its own rules.
- No repeating a comparison because a source makes one. The Pukui and Elbert entry describes kōnane as "resembling checkers". Cite the entry for the rest and leave that phrase out.
- No stereotypes: palm trees, pineapples, hula girls, tiki figures, lei, hibiscus, surfboards, grass skirts, or sunset gradients.
- No "tribal" or faux-kapa typefaces, and no bamboo or carved-wood lettering.
- No petroglyph figures, kapa patterns, or tattoo motifs borrowed as generic decoration. They have meanings and makers. Use one only when it is a specific, sourced design and a practitioner has agreed to the use.
- No names or images of akua (gods) or aliʻi (chiefs) as product names, features, difficulty levels, or mascots.
- No Hawaiian words used for flavor: no "Aloha!" on the title screen, no "Mahalo" on a button.
- No imitation Pidgin.
- No invented traditions, proverbs, or legends. No quoted ʻōlelo noʻeau without the published source and a reviewer's approval.
- No generated "Hawaiian-style" art or music.
- No "ancient", "lost", "exotic", "mystical", or "paradise".

## What we can say

| Statement | Source |
| --- | --- |
| Kōnane is a Hawaiian game played with pebbles set in even lines on a stone or wood board. | Pukui and Elbert, *kōnane*, sense 2 |
| The word kōnane also means bright moonlight, or to shine as the moon. | Pukui and Elbert, *kōnane*, sense 1 |
| A papamū is the stone on which kōnane is played. | Pukui and Elbert, *papamū* |
| ʻIliʻili are pebbles or small stones, as used in kōnane. | Pukui and Elbert, *ʻiliʻili* |
| Kōnane is also the name of a kapa-beater design. | Pukui and Elbert, *kōnane*, sense 3 |
| ʻŌlena, turmeric, was used to color cloth and kapa. | Pukui and Elbert, *ʻōlena* |
| Hawaiian-language newspapers were published from 1834 to 1948. | [nupepa.org](https://nupepa.org) |
| The pieces are black stone and white coral, set in small depressions in the board. | U.S. National Park Service, Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau National Historical Park |
| Stone papamū can be seen at Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau. | U.S. National Park Service |

Dictionary entries are searchable at [wehe.hilo.hawaii.edu](https://wehe.hilo.hawaii.edu/). The park's pages are at [nps.gov/puho](https://www.nps.gov/puho/).

Anything beyond this list needs a source before it ships. That includes who played, when, how old the game is, and how rules varied between islands.

## A note on lua

This project calls a hollow on the board a lua, which means hole or pit. The National Park Service uses poho, a depression or hollow, for the same thing. Keep lua for consistency with the code and [rules.md](../../rules.md), and ask a reviewer which word is better for player-facing text.

## Review

Nothing here has been reviewed by a fluent Hawaiian speaker or a cultural practitioner. The spellings and meanings were checked against the dictionary, which catches spelling errors and misses errors of usage and judgment.

Before a public release, have a qualified reviewer read:

- Every Hawaiian word in the UI and in these documents.
- The glossary in [voice.md](voice.md#which-hawaiian-words-to-use).
- The two descriptions of the game in [voice.md](voice.md#describing-the-game).
- The use of "Play by moonlight" as a line, and of a moonrise on a win.
- The Hawaiian sources given for colors and icons in [visual.md](visual.md).

If a reviewer and this guide disagree, the reviewer is right. Update the guide.
